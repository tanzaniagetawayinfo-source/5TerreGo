Manarola Train Station

Manarola · Cinque Terre e Golfo dei Poeti

Stepping off the train in Manarola means being immediately immersed in a magical atmosphere, where the railway seems wedged between rock walls and the sea. To reach the heart of the village, the only way is through the long pedestrian tunnel: as you walk through, take a moment to observe the mosaics and decorations on the walls that celebrate the lives of local winemakers and the beauty of the landscape. Inside the station, you will find the Park Welcome Center, the ideal reference point for asking about trails, such as the Beccara, or for purchasing the Cinque Terre Card. Thanks to the Card, you can access the station's restrooms for free and use the small buses that circulate within the village. If you arrive with heavy luggage, remember that the station does not have an official storage facility, but you can find convenient private solutions right along the tunnel or on the main street leading down to the sea.

Once you exit the tunnel, you will face a key crossroad for your exploration. If you choose to go right, you will begin the climb toward the upper part of the village, where the Church of San Lorenzo dominates the square and where the steep Beccara Trail begins—the perfect challenge for those who want to reach Riomaggiore on foot. If you continue to the left, the road will lead you gently toward the Marina, the small harbor where colorful boats rest on the cliffs and where the famous panoramic path starts for taking that iconic sunset photo of Manarola. While waiting for your train on the platform, always pay close attention to the yellow line: in this section, long-distance trains often speed through, creating sudden and powerful drafts.

A glimpse into the Station’s Past:

Manarola station is not just a place of transit, but a witness to the resilient history of this village. During World War II, the pedestrian tunnel that today teems with tourists was the safest air-raid shelter for the inhabitants: within those stone-carved walls, fears and hopes were shared during bombings. Before 1874, the year the railway opened, Manarola was a world of its own, accessible only via the grueling steps of the Beccara or by sea. The arrival of the train was met with both wonder and fear, forever changing the fate of the farmers who could finally ship their precious Sciacchetrà wine all over the world. Here’s a final fun fact regarding winter: if you look at the hill above the railway exit, you will see the structures of Mario Andreoli’s illuminated Nativity; the thousands of lights that compose it are connected directly to the electrical lines that power the trains, creating a unique link between modern technology and folk tradition.
