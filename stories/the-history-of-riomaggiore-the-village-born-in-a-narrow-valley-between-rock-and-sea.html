The History of Riomaggiore

The narrow village between rocks and sea

14 min read· 12 May 2026· 5TerreGo

The village born in a narrow valley between rock and sea

Riomaggiore is the first village of the Cinque Terre for those arriving from La Spezia. But calling it “the first” does not do it justice. Riomaggiore is not just the eastern gateway to this stretch of coast. It is one of the places that best explains the vertical soul of the Cinque Terre.

The village seems gathered inside a cut in the mountain. The houses rise one above the other, squeezed along the valley, following the natural slope of the land down to the small landing place by the sea. Visitors today see colours, boats, narrow alleys, stone stairways and sunsets. But behind this image there is a much older story: a story of hilltop settlements, defence, agriculture, the sea, isolation and adaptation.

Riomaggiore was not built to be beautiful. It became beautiful because, for centuries, it had to find an intelligent way to exist in a difficult place.

A name shaped by water

The name Riomaggiore most likely comes from Rivus Major, meaning “major stream” or “large stream”, referring to the watercourse around which the original settlement developed.

Even today, this origin can still be read in the shape of the village. Riomaggiore does not spread out across a wide and comfortable space. It follows a narrow valley, almost hidden, descending towards the sea.

This is essential to understanding the village.

Riomaggiore was not planned like a town on flat land. It grew according to what the landscape allowed. The tall, narrow houses, standing close together along the slope, are not just picturesque. They are a practical answer to the lack of space.

The historic centre developed in the valley of the Rio Maggiore, while the area around the railway station lies in the nearby valley of the Rio Finale. This second area grew especially after the arrival of the railway in the nineteenth century.

That division still tells the story of two Riomaggiores: the old village, enclosed in its original valley, and the modern village, connected to the rest of Liguria by train.

Before the village: life on the heights

As often happens in the history of the Cinque Terre, before the village by the sea there were settlements higher up on the hills.

A local tradition tells an ancient legend: Greek refugees, fleeing the persecutions of the iconoclast emperor Leo III, are said to have landed near Montenero and founded small settlements on the ridges above the coast.

It is a fascinating story, but it should be understood as a legend rather than a historical certainty.

What is more historically plausible is that, in times when the coast was exposed to danger and raids, living higher up was safer than living directly by the sea. Early settlements in this area were often located in elevated positions, connected to defence, territorial control and agriculture.

Only later, when the Ligurian Sea became safer and the power of Genoa became stronger, did people gradually move down towards the coast. From this movement towards the sea, the maritime heart of Riomaggiore began to take shape.

The Middle Ages and the birth of the village

The first reliable historical traces of Riomaggiore become clearer during the Middle Ages.

In the thirteenth century, this part of the coast was involved in the wider political struggles of Liguria. Local feudal families, the Republic of Genoa and rival maritime powers all played a role in shaping the territory.

Riomaggiore was not a large centre. It was a small community in a difficult landscape. But it was not outside history. Its position along the eastern Ligurian coast made it part of a wider network of control, defence and maritime connections.

Around the second half of the thirteenth century, the first defensive structures of the village began to take shape. The castle of Riomaggiore was built in a strategic position above the settlement, on the hill separating the valley of the Rio Maggiore from that of the Rio Finale.

It was not a decorative monument. It was a defensive structure, designed to watch over the territory and protect the community.

Later, under Genoese influence, the defensive system was strengthened. This tells us something important: Riomaggiore may have been small, but it mattered. It belonged to a coast where every harbour, every valley and every lookout point had value.

Genoa and the growth of Riomaggiore

The history of Riomaggiore is closely connected with the Republic of Genoa.

For Genoa, control of the Ligurian coast was essential. The villages of the Cinque Terre were part of a larger system of coastal settlements, sea routes, defensive points and local economies.

Genoese influence brought greater political stability and helped organise the territory. It did not transform Riomaggiore into a rich or powerful town. The village remained small, steep and difficult. But it became more firmly connected to the wider history of Liguria.

During this period, Riomaggiore began to take on a more recognisable form. The houses developed along the valley, the relationship with the sea became more stable, and agricultural and maritime life grew side by side.

The village expanded not through comfort, but through density.

There was little space, so houses rose upwards. Streets became stairways. Alleys followed the slope. The village became almost vertical, built more in height than in width.

The Church of San Giovanni Battista

One of the most important signs of medieval Riomaggiore is the Church of San Giovanni Battista, located in the upper part of the village.

The church was founded in the fourteenth century and remains one of the most meaningful historical landmarks of Riomaggiore. Its position in the higher part of the settlement is not accidental. It reflects a village that was already organised, inhabited and strong enough to build an important religious building.

Today, the church should not be seen only as a monument. It is a visible trace of medieval Riomaggiore: a small community that, despite the limits of the landscape, had developed a clear identity.

It also reminds us that the historic village was not only connected to the sea. Its life extended upwards, towards the slopes, the paths and the cultivated land above the houses.

An agricultural village before a tourist destination

Looking at Riomaggiore today, it is easy to think mainly of the sea.

The marina, the boats, the rocks and the sunsets are among the most recognisable images of the village. But for centuries, Riomaggiore was also, and perhaps above all, an agricultural village.

Families worked the hills around the village, using every possible piece of land. Vineyards played a central role, as they did throughout the Cinque Terre.

This subject deserves its own article, because to truly explain agriculture in Riomaggiore means speaking about terracing, dry-stone walls, heroic viticulture, wine and the human transformation of the landscape.

Here, it is enough to understand one thing: the landscape around Riomaggiore is not simply natural. It is the result of centuries of human work.

The mountain was not only inhabited. It was shaped.

The paths that visitors walk today were once working routes. The climbs were not scenic experiences. They were part of daily life. Beauty was not the goal. It was the consequence.

The sea: work, connection and danger

The sea has always had an ambiguous role in the history of Riomaggiore.

On one side, it was a resource. It offered fish, movement, exchange and contact with other coastal communities. In a territory where travelling by land was slow and difficult, the sea was often the most natural road.

On the other side, the sea was also a danger.

For centuries, the Ligurian coast had to deal with raids, wars, rival powers and the constant need for defence. The position of the castle and the defensive structures of the village reflect this double condition.

Riomaggiore looked towards the sea, but it also had to protect itself from it.

This explains much of the character of the village. Riomaggiore is open and closed at the same time. Open towards the horizon, but enclosed within its valley. Maritime, but deeply tied to the mountain. Visible from the sea, yet protected by rock.

From isolation to the railway

For centuries, Riomaggiore was difficult to reach.

Land connections were tiring. The paths required time, effort and local knowledge. The landscape imposed a kind of natural isolation on the village.

The great turning point came with the railway.

The construction of the railway line along the Ligurian coast in the nineteenth century changed the destiny of Riomaggiore and of the Cinque Terre. The village slowly emerged from its historic isolation. People could move more easily. Goods could circulate more regularly. The village became connected to a wider world.

The area around the station, developed in the valley of the Rio Finale, is the physical symbol of this transformation.

On one side, there is the old village, grown along the Rio Maggiore. On the other, there is the modern Riomaggiore, opened by the train.

Without the railway, Riomaggiore would probably not have become what it is today: one of the most visited and recognisable villages in Liguria.

The nineteenth and twentieth centuries

After the fall of the Republic of Genoa, Riomaggiore followed the broader political history of Liguria: the Napoleonic period, the Ligurian Republic, French rule, the Kingdom of Sardinia and finally the Kingdom of Italy from 1861.

These political changes may seem distant from the daily life of a small village, but they marked Riomaggiore’s entry into the modern administrative world.

The village was no longer only a small coastal community tied to Genoa. It became part of a national state, with new infrastructures, new rules and new connections.

In the twentieth century, the transformation accelerated. The railway, and later the road connection, brought Riomaggiore closer to the rest of Liguria.

The isolation that had shaped the village for centuries began to fade.

Riomaggiore and the birth of its tourist image

Tourism arrived slowly at first, then more quickly.

Visitors began to look at Riomaggiore with different eyes from those of the people who lived there. What had once been ordinary, or even difficult, suddenly became fascinating: the stairways, the alleys, the tall houses, the narrow valley, the harbour, the paths, the dramatic meeting of rock and sea.

The village, born from necessity, became a landscape to admire.

This is one of the keys to understanding the Cinque Terre. The villages were not built to look romantic. They became romantic because they preserved forms created by real needs: defence, space, work, movement and survival.

Riomaggiore is one of the strongest examples of this transformation. Its vertical structure, once a practical answer to a difficult territory, is now exactly what makes it unforgettable to visitors from all over the world.

Riomaggiore and the Via dell’Amore

It is impossible to speak about Riomaggiore without mentioning the Via dell’Amore, the famous path connecting the village to Manarola.

But this subject deserves its own article.

For Riomaggiore, the Via dell’Amore represents an important chapter because it changed the relationship between two neighbouring villages. It made a connection that had once required more difficult routes feel direct, symbolic and emotional.

Over time, the Via dell’Amore became one of the most famous places in the Cinque Terre, linking Riomaggiore to an international romantic imagination.

Its history, closures, restoration and reopening also show a very modern challenge: how to protect a fragile place that the whole world wants to see.

Riomaggiore and the UNESCO recognition

In 1997, the area of Porto Venere, the Cinque Terre and the islands of Palmaria, Tino and Tinetto was added to the UNESCO World Heritage List.

Riomaggiore is part of this recognition not only because it is beautiful, but because it contributes to a wider cultural landscape.

The value of this area lies in the relationship between human settlements, natural environment, traditional agriculture, sea and mountain. Riomaggiore is one of the clearest examples of that relationship.

The village is not important only because it is photogenic. It is important because it shows how a community adapted to a difficult territory without completely erasing its character.

The houses, the valley, the sea, the paths, the cultivated slopes, the castle and the church are not separate elements. Together, they form a historical landscape.

Riomaggiore today

Today, Riomaggiore is one of the most visited villages in the Cinque Terre.

For many travellers, it is the first encounter with this coastline. You arrive by train, leave the station, walk towards the centre, and suddenly the village opens in front of you, descending towards the sea.

But Riomaggiore should not be visited only as a photo stop.

To understand it, you have to look at its shape.

The narrow valley. The tall houses. The upper part of the village. The castle. The church. The small harbour. The climbs. The constant relationship between the inland valley and the sea.

Every detail tells an old choice: to build where there was little space, to live where it was difficult, to resist between rock and water.

Why Riomaggiore is different

Riomaggiore has a very specific identity.

Monterosso is wider and more open. Vernazza turns around its natural harbour. Corniglia dominates the sea from above. Manarola seems carved into the rock.

Riomaggiore, instead, is the village of the valley.

It descends towards the sea like a corridor of houses, shadow, colour and stone. It is perhaps the village that most clearly shows the vertical tension of the Cinque Terre.

You do not understand Riomaggiore only with your eyes. You understand it with your body.

You climb. You descend. You turn into narrow alleys. You feel the slope beneath your feet.

That physical experience is part of its identity. Riomaggiore is not comfortable, and it does not pretend to be. Its beauty comes from the fact that it still preserves the shape of the effort that built it.

Conclusion

The history of Riomaggiore is the story of adaptation.

First, settlements on the heights. Then, the gradual movement towards the sea. First, defence. Then, trade and connection. First, isolation. Then, the railway. First, daily work. Then, the gaze of travellers.

Riomaggiore did not become famous because someone designed it to impress visitors. It became famous because its shape tells something authentic: the way a community learned to live in a difficult place, transforming a narrow valley into an unforgettable village.

Anyone passing through Riomaggiore should remember this.

Behind every photograph, there is a longer story. Behind every alley, there is a necessity. Behind every climb, there is an ancient way of inhabiting the world.

Riomaggiore is not only the first village of the Cinque Terre.

It is a gateway into their deepest history.
